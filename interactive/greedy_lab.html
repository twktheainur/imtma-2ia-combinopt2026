A fair empirical comparison
Which greedy rule survives a full route?
Run all six rules from an empty solution with the same satellite plan. Inspect the result that surprised you, then return to the editor to understand the individual choices.






Keep one decision constant
For a given instance and satellite rule, customer assignments and the initial main route are identical across all six policies. Only the order in which customers fill the truck trips changes. New trips start when no remaining assigned customer fits.
Read the reference correctly
“Best observed” means the best feasible result in this experiment. It is not a proven optimum or a course ranking mark. The browser experiment is constructive and deterministic; use the Python harness for the course’s 30-second evaluation.








Look for exceptions, instance by instance
A policy’s average can hide a poor result on a particular geometry. Costs are compared within each instance before aggregation; each instance has equal weight.


What do the satellite rules do?
One satellite: best star estimate
Try every satellite in a quick estimate: the depot’s round trip plus a separate satellite–customer–satellite trip for every customer. Choose the least costly estimate, then build real multi-customer trips there. This is an assignment heuristic, not a proof that one satellite is sufficient for the best solution.
Nearest satellite
Assign each customer to its nearest eligible satellite. “All” uses every satellite as a candidate; “my visited satellites” uses the current manual main route and is available for the current instance. The main route is built by cheapest insertion, with deterministic ties.






A construction method you can explain
Learn what the indicators are telling you
The lab is a place to form a hypothesis, inspect its consequences, and turn it into an algorithm. A useful greedy policy has a clear next-move rule, respects feasibility, and performs consistently enough to be a sensible starting solution.


Three objects, two kinds of movement
  1. The main depot is the start and finish.The truck and trailer leave together and return together. Their main route can visit satellite depots.
  2. A satellite is a parking and reload point.The trailer stays there while the truck serves customers. Several truck trips may leave the same satellite. They are executed there before the main route continues; trip numbers are labels, not repeated satellite visits.
  3. A truck trip is a closed customer sequence.It starts and finishes at the same satellite. Each customer appears in exactly one trip over the whole solution.
Two capacity checks with different meanings
For each truck trip, the sum of customer demands must be at most the truck capacity. Returning to the satellite allows the truck to reload for another trip.
Across the whole instance, total demand must fit in the combined truck and trailer capacities. There is no additional capacity limit on the total demand assigned to a satellite in this course model.
Do not treat “truck capacity” as a limit on every customer assigned to one satellite. It limits one trip at a time.




The objective
Count every travelled leg
The objective is the distance of the main truck-and-trailer route plus the distance of every closed truck trip.
C = Cmain + ∑ Ctrip
The return to a satellite counts even while you are building a trip. The map shows that closing leg so that every proposed addition has an honest marginal cost.
In the worked example: the main route 0 → 1 → 2 → 0 costs 16. The three truck trips cost about 8.606, 8.000 and 8.944. Their complete solution costs about 41.550.
This is a feasible reference design. Its presence in the lab does not establish optimality.


The next move
Include the return leg in Δ
Let s be the active satellite, t the last customer already in the trip, and i a candidate. When the trip is empty, t = s.
Δ(i) = d(t, i) + d(i, s) − d(t, s)
You add the legs t → i and i → s, then remove the old closing leg t → s. The nearest candidate minimizes only the first term.
Try it: from satellite 1, add customer 3. Adding customer 4 next replaces a return of 3 by legs of 2 and √13.
Δ = 2 + √13 − 3 ≈ 2.606. Its demand 4 exactly fills the remaining capacity after customer 3’s demand 6.


The resource tradeoff
Cheap distance can consume useful capacity
The load after adding customer i is current load + qi. A locally attractive small customer can prevent a larger customer from fitting later; a large customer can also block a useful nearby combination.
The ratio Δ / qi asks how much extra distance you pay per unit delivered. It can favor large demands. The largest-demand rule directly prioritizes packing difficult customers early.
Higher average load does not guarantee lower distance. Use it to explain a result, alongside trip count and geometry.


The location tradeoff
A closer satellite also has a main-route cost
Moving customers to a nearby satellite can shorten their truck trips. Activating that satellite can lengthen the route driven with the trailer.
The manual editor shows the additional main-route cost before adding a new satellite. In policy comparisons, customer assignments and the main route are held fixed so you can study the customer-ordering rule on its own.
After comparing customer rules, repeat the experiment with a different satellite assignment. Change one mechanism at a time so you can explain the effect.




Six useful hypotheses to test
Each rule chooses among customers that fit in the active trip. The same local criterion is applied again after every accepted customer; a new trip may change the ordering.
RuleWhat it prefersWhat to look for in the resulting routes
Nearest neighbourSmallest distance from the current tail: d(t, i)Short successive legs, but possibly an expensive closing leg or distant customers left until the end.
Cheapest additionSmallest increase in the closed trip: Δ(i)A good immediate cost increase. Check whether it leaves awkward demands or isolated customers for later trips.
Cost per unit demandSmallest Δ(i) / qiEfficient delivery of demand. Inspect whether favorable ratios pull the route away from a compact cluster.
Largest demand firstLargest demand qiEarly placement of large customers. Count the resulting trips and inspect the geographical detours.
Farthest from satelliteLargest root distance d(s, i)Distant customers handled early. See whether nearer customers can share those long trips efficiently.
Savings firstLargest saving against a separate singleton trip: d(s, i) + d(i, s) − Δ(i)Customers whose separate out-and-back trip is expensive compared with appending them here. On the symmetric benchmark the singleton cost is 2d(s, i). This is an append-based savings rule, not the full Clarke–Wright merge algorithm.
A rule’s label is less precise than its formula. State the formula, capacity filter, tie-breaking rule, satellite assignment, and stopping rule in your method description.


Turn an observation into an experiment
Use the table, map and saved designs to produce an explanation that another team could reproduce.
A short empirical worksheet
  1. Write a prediction.For example: “Using added distance per unit demand will reduce the number of trips on this instance, but may increase detours.”
  2. Trace a few decisions manually.Compare two rules at the same partial solution. Record the selected customer, added distance and remaining capacity. Explain why the rules disagree.
  3. Compare complete solutions.Run the six policies with one fixed satellite rule. Inspect total distance, the main/truck split, trip count and load utilization together.
  4. Find a counterexample.Run all public instances and inspect a case where your preferred rule loses. Look at the geometry and the last trips it creates.
  5. Change one mechanism.Try another satellite assignment or a clearly defined tie-break. Save the original design so you can identify what changed.
  6. Choose and justify a baseline.State where the rule performs well, where it struggles, and which local-search move could repair a typical weakness.
Read relative results carefully. A percentage above the best observed cost compares only the methods in the current experiment. It is not an optimality gap. Do not average raw distances across differently sized instances; compare within each instance, then aggregate those relative comparisons with equal instance weights.

What the picture can and cannot tell you
The map uses the instance’s coordinates with the same scale on both axes. Calculations use the declared distance metric. For an explicit symmetric distance matrix, the drawn geometry may not represent the numerical travel costs.
Displayed values are rounded for reading. Decisions and the exported route are evaluated using the underlying distances. Coincident nodes remain separate customers and can be selected in the table.
